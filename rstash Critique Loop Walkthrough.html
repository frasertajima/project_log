The loop in one screen

rstash
Ask a question.
c: <critique>
Criticise the last answer. A multi-line paste is fine.
rstash --research
Look for outside sources (arXiv, OpenAlex) for points the library can't settle.
rstash --review
Approve or reject each source passage, with a reason. The answers are then re-checked.
rstash --gaps
Re-ask where an answer ignored evidence it was given.
rstash --verdicts
Override a verdict yourself, with a reason.
rstash --revise
Correct the critique where it claimed more than any source shows.

Behind c:, rstash takes four steps:

  1. It splits the critique into points. Each point is a claim (you assert something) or a question (you ask or expect something).
  2. It writes one neutral follow-up question per point. The question names the subject in full and never assumes your answer.
  3. It answers each follow-up in research mode: the usual semantic search, plus the pages most relevant to the question and some model-written search terms, found by exact keyword search over whole pages.
  4. An independent judge rules each point:
VerdictMeaning
answeredYour sources, cited in the answer, settle the point. A question can be settled explicitly or by inference.
partly supportedThe sources back part of it. The report says what they establish, where the critique went further than any source, and any answer gap (evidence that was in front of the answer but went unused).
not settledYour sources don't settle it. Research is the next step.

Each critique is a session, a folder holding the critique, the follow-up plan, the results with their history, any overrides, and a REPORT.md. Approved sources sit in a separate "approved additions" overlay next to the main index. Their citations carry [added], so every answer shows which parts rest on sources you approved.

Example 1: "Hamiltonian Monte Carlo use in machine learning"

Step 1: the first answer

$ rstash
Question: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo use in machine learning

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), also referred to as Hybrid Monte Carlo [algorithms_for_valuation.pdf p.168], is a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method that utilizes Hamiltonian dynamics … It is used to sample from complex, high-dimensional, and multimodal distributions … HMC uses a simulation of Hamiltonian dynamics based on the gradient of the log-likelihood … symplectic integrators … ensure stability …

The audit line looked perfect: answer 12 claims, 12 cited · citations match excerpt labels. But every claim being cited doesn't make the answer correct.

  • A reviewer found one misleading claim (multimodality presented as a strength), one imprecision ("log-likelihood" instead of the log target density) and one major omission (the Metropolis accept/reject step).
  • 8 of the 15 excerpts came from a single blog post. The textbook pages on HMC never reached the context.
  • Against 12 checks drawn from that critique, the original answer passed 0–1 of 12.

Step 3: find outside sources

For points the library can't settle, rstash takes these steps:

  • It writes search queries for arXiv and OpenAlex, and screens titles and abstracts.
  • It downloads the best papers and picks their most relevant pages.
  • It quotes the deciding sentences, checked word for word against the page. A quote that can't be found verbatim is dropped.

It searches for the part the critique claimed beyond the sources, not for the whole claim.

$ rstash --research
research 1 point(s): P2
P2: 13 hits → Zhang et al. (2023) · Du & Mordatch (2019), Implicit Generation and Generalization in EBMs · Nie et al. (2021) ...
  arXiv_1903.08689 p.3: PARTIAL We note that HMC [Neal, 2011] may be an even more efficient gradient algorithm ...
  arXiv_2110.10873 p.2: PARTIAL For sampling, most existing EBMs rely on the Langevin dynamics (LD) sampling ...

Step 5: close answer gaps

Sometimes the evidence is in front of the answer and it doesn't use it. --gaps re-asks those follow-ups with a note that points at the unused excerpt. It adds no new facts, and the trace records the exact prompt.

$ rstash --gaps
### Beyond stability, what roles do volume preservation and time reversibility play ...  · with the answer-gap note
  C4: partly supported → answered
### What are the computational costs associated with gradient evaluations per leapfrog step ...
  C6b: partly supported → answered

That took the session from 7 to 9 answered. The three points left were all cases of the critique claiming more than any source shows: "usually" (C5), an explicit statement about Neal's funnel (C6c), and "default" in PyMC and NumPyro (C6d).

Step 6: correct the critique

Critiques can over-claim too. --revise shows each over-reaching claim, what the sources establish, and a draft rewrite. You accept the draft, write your own, or keep the original. The existing answers are then re-judged against the revised claims.

  original claim: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo struggles when the curvature of the target varies, such as Neal's funnel

  curvature, which are characteristic of hierarchical models; Neal's funnel is a standard hierarchical test case for this.

  C5: partly supported → answered
  C6c: partly supported → answered
  C6d: partly supported → answered

C5EBMs are usually sampled with Langevin dynamics …Langevin dynamics, the one-leapfrog-step special case of HMC, is a popular, state-of-the-art sampler for training EBMs, alongside full HMC.

Review the drafts rather than accepting them blindly. In both HMC rounds the model's drafts over-corrected: one dropped a point the sources do support, and another softened "state-of-the-art" to "often". The review screen shows what the sources establish so you can judge.

Step 7 (optional): override a verdict

$ rstash --verdicts
P1 judge: partial · plain HMC is bad at multimodal targets ...
verdict? (a[: reason], r, k, q): a: the hedged version is what I meant

The report then reads "answered by reviewer (the hedged version is what I meant; judge: partly supported)". Your view and the judge's view both stay visible.

Before and after

On multimodality, the first answer said:

It is used to sample from complex, high-dimensional, and multimodal distributions …

The final follow-up answer, with sources:

HMC may not converge well when potential energy has multiple minima separated by large barriers [Bou-Rabee & Sanz-Serna 2018, p.84]. This is because momentum randomizations may fail to produce initial conditions with enough kinetic energy to cross potential energy barriers or may lack the correct direction to exit a current potential well [Bou-Rabee & Sanz-Serna 2018, p.84] … For highly multimodal targets, HMC often does not beat RWM because the gradient within one mode is effectively ignorant of other modes [Mangoubi et al. 2018, p.1] … HMC can be used in conjunction with tempering techniques …

Example 2: "Can the Toyota bZ lock itself when I walk away?"

A practical question about a 729-page owner's manual. Two Nissan Ariya manuals share the same index.

Step 1: ask

$ rstash
Question: can the toyota bz automatically lock the doors when the driver walks away from the car?

Yes, the Toyota bZ has a "close & lock (Walk-Away) function," though this setting must be customized at a Toyota dealer [2027-Toyota-bZ.pdf p.175]. To use this function, the user must:

  1. Close all doors except the back door while carrying an electronic key.
  2. Press the switch on the lower part of the back door, which triggers a specific buzzer and puts the function into standby.
  3. Move away from the back door while the buzzer is sounding [p.175].

… all doors other than the back door will lock, and the back door will lock once it has closed [p.176].

That's an interesting find: a walk-away function exists, but it starts at the back door. Is it really automatic?

Step 2: criticise it

c: this still requires manual button pressing by the user (and being at the back door); The Ariya enables
   automatic locking of all doors by walking away from the car. It seems the bz does not?

P1 claim     The Toyota bZ 'close & lock (Walk-Away) function' requires the user to manually press a button at the back door.
P2 claim     The Nissan Ariya enables automatic locking of all doors by walking away from the car.
P3 question  Does the Toyota bZ have a fully automatic walk-away door locking feature that does not require manual button pressing?
PointVerdictWhat rstash found
P1answered"requires the user to press the switch on the lower part of the back door while carrying an electronic key and having all other doors closed" (p.175)
P2partly supportedThe Ariya has a "Walk away lock function" (2024 manual p.277), but no excerpt describes what it does.
P3answered, inferredNo. "The Toyota bZ's close & lock (Walk-Away) function must be started by pressing the switch on the lower part of the back door, and it must be enabled by a Toyota dealer."

P3 shows why an answer settled by inference is useful. The manual never says the bZ cannot lock itself, because manuals describe what a car does. rstash reports the conclusion the pages support, labels it as inferred, and shows the reasoning.

Step 3: read "partly supported" carefully

For P2, the follow-up retrieved only the Ariya manual's contents entry (p.277), not the page that describes the feature. The verdict reflects a retrieval miss inside the library, not missing knowledge. A narrower question finds the page:

$ rstash "What does the Nissan Ariya walk away lock function do?"

The Nissan Ariya walk away lock function automatically locks the vehicle when you walk away from it with the Intelligent Key, with an operation range of approximately 7 ft (2 m) [2026-Nissan-Ariya-TypeD-OM-1st.pdf p.274]. When the vehicle is locked via this function, the hazard indicator lights flash twice … This function is disabled by default, but it can be enabled through the vehicle information display …

To record that conclusion in the session, rstash --verdicts lets you accept P2 with a reason, for example a: Ariya manual p.274 describes it; asked directly.

The answer

  • Toyota bZ: walk-away locking exists, but it is not fully automatic. A Toyota dealer has to enable it, and each time you start it by pressing the switch under the back door (pp.175–176).
  • Nissan Ariya: fully automatic once switched on in the vehicle information display, where it is off by default. It locks when you walk more than about 7 ft (2 m) away with the key (2026 manual p.274).

This matches what owners report: the bZ unlocks itself as you approach but doesn't lock as you leave, while the Ariya does both. The bZ's regenerative-braking level follows the same pattern. It isn't remembered between drives, a conclusion settled by inference in an earlier session from the manual's cancellation rules (pp.244–245).

What the two examples show

  • The critique step corrects retrieval by itself. Naming the subject in every follow-up and adding exact keyword search over whole pages brought in pages the first, naive question missed.
  • "Not settled" and "partly supported" are informative. They say exactly what is missing: a source (research), unused evidence (--gaps), a claim that went too far (--revise), or a page the library has but didn't retrieve (ask more narrowly).
  • You decide what counts as true. New sources enter only through review, with your reasons on record. Overrides are labelled as yours, and the critique itself can be corrected.
  • Every result is traceable. Each answer, judgement and decision is written to the session folder and the audit log.

rstash is a pure-Rust retrieval tool: GPU-exact search over SEC filings, PDFs, transcripts, web clippings, image descriptions and Kindle highlights, with a local or cloud language model for answers and a tool-less Claude call as the independent judge.
